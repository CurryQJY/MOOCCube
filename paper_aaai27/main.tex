\title{Course-Knowledge-Guided Reinforcement Learning for Strict Course-Cold MOOC Recommendation}
\author{Anonymous Submission}
\affiliations{}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
MOOC platforms must rank newly released courses before those courses collect interactions, but standard offline protocols can make this setting appear easier than deployed full-catalog ranking. Sampled candidates remove many warm competitors, and interaction-weighted averages can be driven by a few frequently requested cold courses. We study strict course-cold MOOC recommendation, where target courses have zero training interactions, compete against the full catalog, and are evaluated by course-macro Recall/NDCG. The benchmark asks whether each zero-interaction course, rather than only each cold-course interaction, receives early exposure while remaining pedagogically plausible. We propose Course-Knowledge-Guided Reinforcement Learning (CKG-RL), which anchors course representations in content, masks cold-course ID evidence, refines course states through retrieval-based learner simulation, and uses concept, prerequisite, difficulty, and redundancy signals in sampling and rewards. On MOOCCube, Junyi, and COCO, CKG-RL has the highest mean in every @5/@10 column, with relative gains from +3.5\% to +33.1\%; Holm-corrected paired tests support 11 of 12 early-rank comparisons. MOOCCube ablations attribute the gains to educational rewards, knowledge-guided sampling, and prerequisite supervision. These results suggest that strict course-cold ranking benefits from making educational constraints part of candidate construction and reward design, rather than treating course knowledge only as side features.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
MOOC platforms continually add courses, course versions, and learning paths. Recommenders therefore must rank courses before interaction histories are available, a persistent cold-start condition~\cite{schein2002coldstart,gantner2010attribute,lin2024tdro,zhang2025coldstartllm}. In education, cold-start ranking is not only a sparsity problem. A useful course should be topically relevant, prerequisite-safe, difficulty-appropriate, and non-redundant with prior learning. Public MOOC resources expose such course-side signals through metadata, concepts, and prerequisite relations~\cite{yu2020mooccube,wang2021mooccubex}.

Common offline protocols can hide this risk. Sampled candidate sets may remove the warm courses that dominate the real catalog~\cite{krichene2020sampled}, and reproducibility studies show that weak protocols and baselines can overstate progress~\cite{dacrema2019progress}. Interaction-weighted averages may also be driven by a few high-volume cold courses, echoing recent concerns that mean ranking utility can conceal tail-risk failures~\cite{takehi2025diversification}.

We study \emph{strict course-cold MOOC recommendation}: target courses have zero training interactions, compete against the full catalog, and are evaluated by course-macro Recall/NDCG. The evaluation unit is the cold course itself rather than a cold-course interaction drawn from a popular course. This benchmark forces the model to answer two questions at once: whether a new course can obtain early exposure at all, and whether the exposed course is educationally plausible for the learner.

Figure~\ref{fig:method-motivation} makes this motivation concrete on MOOCCube. The closest content-graph baseline, CGRC, still leaves 46\% of seed-course cases with NDCG@10 no greater than 0.10 under full-catalog course-cold ranking. The same cold-test setting also exhibits large prerequisite and difficulty gaps and sparse concept continuity, indicating that cold MOOC recommendation must address both exposure and pedagogical fit. These diagnostics motivate placing course knowledge inside retrieval, sampling, and reward design rather than treating it as a passive course feature.

CKG-RL uses course knowledge in four places: content anchoring, cold-course ID masking, retrieval-based learner simulation, and rewards over concepts, prerequisites, difficulty, and redundancy. Educational knowledge therefore affects the ranking dynamics instead of appearing only as appended course features.

Across MOOCCube, Junyi, and COCO, CKG-RL has higher @10 course-macro means than the strongest non-CKG baseline under strict course-cold full-ranking evaluation. We restrict this conclusion to early-rank exposure for zero-interaction courses. Some baselines remain competitive on broader or hot-course diagnostics.

This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We define a strict course-cold MOOC benchmark with full-catalog course-macro Recall/NDCG as the primary target.
    \item We introduce CKG-RL, which combines content anchoring, forced-cold masking, retrieval-based simulation, and educational rewards.
    \item We evaluate three datasets against collaborative, graph, content-transfer, and cold-start baselines; ablations isolate the course-knowledge components.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
Educational recommendation must match resources with pedagogical context and learning paths~\cite{dasilva2023educationalrs,rahayu2023learningpath}. MOOCCube and MOOCCubeX provide interactions, course metadata, concepts, teachers, schools, and prerequisite relations~\cite{yu2020mooccube,wang2021mooccubex}. Prior MOOC recommenders have used sequential decisions and explainable path reasoning~\cite{zhang2019hrlcourse,dessi2024findingpaths}. For course-cold targets, these course-side signals remain available even though target-course behavior is absent. The available evidence is therefore course-side. We treat concepts, prerequisites, difficulty, and redundancy as evidence for cold-course ranking.

Cold-start recommendation maps side attributes and content into collaborative spaces when observations are sparse~\cite{schein2002coldstart,gantner2010attribute,liu2024once,wu2024llmrec,zhang2025coldstartllm}. Interaction-only and graph recommenders such as BPR, NGCF, and LightGCN~\cite{rendle2009bpr,wang2019ngcf,he2020lightgcn} are strong when enough edges are observed, but course cold-start requires ranking targets whose IDs provide little supervised signal. Recent methods transfer warm-course information through dropout, contrastive alignment, distillation, graph reconstruction, LLM augmentation or simulation, OOV refinement, structure completion, meta-learning, or exploration~\cite{volkovs2017dropoutnet,zhou2023ccfcrec,huang2023aldi,kim2024cgrc,wang2024llmaugmenter,huang2025coldllm,zhao2024usim,zhao2025fsgnn,luo2025pam,jiang2025itemcentric}. These mechanisms differ, but they share a transfer view of course cold-start. Our question is whether such transfer remains effective when cold targets face the full catalog and must satisfy educational constraints.

Offline recommender evaluation is sensitive to metric choice, candidate construction, and reproducibility practice~\cite{sun2020rigorous,hidasi2023flaws,bauer2024landscape}. Sampled negatives can change comparisons relative to full ranking~\cite{krichene2020sampled,li2024itemsampling}, while distribution-aware evaluation exposes variation across poorly served groups or courses~\cite{ekstrand2024distributional}. We apply this concern to course-cold recommendation: the unit at risk is a held-out course, and the failure is no useful exposure under full-catalog ranking~\cite{takehi2025diversification}. Reinforcement-learning recommenders model sequential decisions~\cite{shani2005mdp,zhao2018deepreinforcement,chen2023rlrecsurvey}. CKG-RL uses a narrower simulator for learner-course matching under course-cold evidence constraints.

\section{Problem Formulation}

\subsection{Preliminaries}
Let \(\mathcal{U}\) denote the set of learners and \(\mathcal{I}\) denote the set of courses. Each observed interaction is represented as \((u,c,t)\), where learner \(u \in \mathcal{U}\) interacted with course \(c \in \mathcal{I}\) at time \(t\). Given the training interactions \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}\) and course-side information, a recommender produces, for each learner \(u\), a ranked list \(\pi_u\) over candidate courses.

Each course \(c\) has a content vector \(\mathbf{x}_c\) and educational signals. We use concept overlap, prerequisite links, training popularity, and history similarity as proxies for topical continuity, readiness, difficulty, and redundancy, consistent with pedagogical-fit views of educational recommendation~\cite{dasilva2023educationalrs,rahayu2023learningpath}. These signals are available even when \(c\) has no training interactions. They are not complete learner-state measurements.

\subsection{Strict Course-Cold Protocol}
We study a course-cold held-out setting. The course set is partitioned into warm courses \(\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{warm}}\) and cold test courses \(\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cold}}\). A cold test course satisfies
\begin{equation}
|\{(u',c',t)\in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}: c'=c\}|=0.
\end{equation}
Thus the model cannot use interaction-trained evidence for the target course ID. Its representation must come from content and educational knowledge.

For a test pair \((u,c^+)\) with \(c^+\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cold}}\), the candidate set is the full catalog after removing courses already observed in the learner's training history:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_u=\mathcal{I}\setminus \mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{train}}_u .
\end{equation}
The model scores every course in \(\mathcal{C}_u\), avoiding sampled-candidate artifacts~\cite{krichene2020sampled,li2024itemsampling} and weak-protocol effects that can distort recommender comparisons~\cite{dacrema2019progress,hidasi2023flaws}. The split is course-based rather than user-based: test learners have training histories, while target courses have none.

\subsection{Standard Interaction Average}
Let \(m_K(u,c^+)\) denote a top-\(K\) utility for a test pair, instantiated as Recall@\(K\) or NDCG@\(K\). Let \(r_{u,c^+}\) be the rank of target course \(c^+\) in \(\pi_u\). Recall@\(K\) equals one when \(r_{u,c^+}\le K\) and zero otherwise, and NDCG@\(K\) is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{NDCG@K}(u,c^+)=
\begin{cases}
1/\log_2(r_{u,c^+}+1), & r_{u,c^+}\le K,\\
0, & r_{u,c^+}>K.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Since each test case contains one held-out positive course, these metrics measure whether the target cold course is surfaced early in the full ranking.

A common evaluation choice is to average the utility over cold-course test interactions:
\begin{equation}
M_{\mathrm{micro}}(K)=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{cold}}_{\mathrm{test}}|}
\sum_{(u,c^+)\in \mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{cold}}_{\mathrm{test}}}
m_K(u,c^+).
\end{equation}
This measures interaction-level utility, but it does not show whether each cold course receives exposure.

\subsection{Cold-Course Risk Metric}
Strict course-cold recommendation has a course-level failure mode: high-volume cold courses can dominate the average while other cold courses remain unseen. To expose this tail risk and distributional variation~\cite{ekstrand2024distributional,takehi2025diversification}, we aggregate first within each cold course. Let the scalar course-level quality at cutoff \(K\) be
\begin{equation}
q_c(K)=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}(c)|}
\sum_{(u,c)\in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}(c)}
m_K(u,c),
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}(c)\) is the set of test cases whose target course is \(c\). The course-macro metric is then
\begin{equation}
M_{\mathrm{macro}}(K)=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cold}}|}
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cold}}} q_c(K).
\end{equation}
Micro averaging asks whether a random cold-course interaction is served well. Course-macro averaging asks whether a random cold course is served well. We use course-macro as the primary metric because the unit of failure is a zero-interaction course receiving no useful full-catalog rank.

\subsection{Main Objective}
The objective is to learn \(s(u,c)\) that ranks \(c^+\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cold}}\) highly within \(\mathcal{C}_u\), using learner history and course-side evidence but no interaction-derived target-course signal. Hot-course metrics are diagnostics for the trade-off between zero-interaction generalization and warm-course ranking.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Overview}
CKG-RL makes the evidence boundary of strict course-cold ranking explicit. For each learner-course pair \((u,c)\), the model builds a learner representation \(\mathbf{z}_u\) from the learner history and an initial course embedding \(\mathbf{e}_c\) from course-side evidence. A simulator starts from \(\mathbf{e}_c\), refines it through \(T\) learner-matching steps, and returns the final course embedding \(\mathbf{z}_{c,T}:=\mathbf{h}_{T,c}\). The ranking score then matches \(\mathbf{z}_{c,T}\) with the target learner representation.

Three design constraints connect the architecture to the two-part motivation. Content anchoring and forced masking enforce the zero-interaction evidence boundary. Simulation exposes cold representations to plausible learner states under full-catalog competition. Course knowledge then filters matches that are semantically related but prerequisite-unsafe, too difficult, or redundant. Figure~\ref{fig:ckg-rl-framework} summarizes the training and evaluation flow.

\subsection{Content-Anchored Course Representation}
The content branch provides the evidence available for a course with zero training interactions, a role emphasized in content-based and LLM-assisted recommendation~\cite{liu2024once,wu2024llmrec}. Each learner \(u\) has a trainable ID embedding \(\mathbf{e}_u\), projected into the recommendation space:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_u=f_u(\mathbf{e}_u).
\end{equation}
Each course \(c\) has a trainable ID embedding \(\mathbf{v}_c\) and a frozen 768-dimensional content embedding \(\mathbf{x}_c\). A content projection network maps \(\mathbf{x}_c\) into the same latent space:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{c}_c=f_c(\mathbf{x}_c).
\end{equation}
The initial course embedding is a gated fusion of the projected content vector and a possibly masked ID vector:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{g}_c=\sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_g[\bar{\mathbf{v}}_c;\mathbf{c}_c]\right),
\quad
\mathbf{e}_c=\mathbf{g}_c\odot \bar{\mathbf{v}}_c+(1-\mathbf{g}_c)\odot \mathbf{c}_c.
\end{equation}
Here \(\bar{\mathbf{v}}_c\) is the ID branch after masking and ID dropout. The gate lets warm courses use collaborative evidence when available, while the content branch defines \(\mathbf{e}_c\) when ID evidence is unavailable.

\subsection{Forced-Cold ID Masking}
Forced-cold masking enforces the protocol inside the model. Let \(p_c\) be the training-set popularity of course \(c\). Under the strict course-cold protocol, a course is cold when \(p_c<1\), which is equivalent to having zero training interactions. For cold examples, CKG-RL removes the course ID evidence:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\mathbf{v}}_c=(1-\xi_c)\mathbf{v}_c,
\quad
\xi_c=\mathbb{I}[p_c<1].
\end{equation}
During training, ordinary ID dropout is additionally applied with probability \(0.35\). At cold-course evaluation time, the target course vector is also built with the ID branch masked. This keeps the representation used at inference aligned with the evidence available in the strict course-cold protocol.

\subsection{Retrieval-Based User Simulation}
Full-catalog course-cold ranking requires a course representation that can compete for plausible learners, not only a pointwise content score. Like recent augmentation or simulation approaches~\cite{wang2024llmaugmenter,huang2025coldllm}, CKG-RL compensates for missing course interactions while keeping the simulator bounded. It initializes \(\mathbf{h}_{0,c}=\mathbf{e}_c\). At step \(t\), it retrieves candidate learners from a normalized user bank by cosine similarity to \(\mathbf{h}_{t,c}\). Let \(\mathcal{N}_M(\mathbf{h}_{t,c})\) be the top-\(M\) retrieved learners. CKG-RL samples \(N\) candidates from this set with a temperature-smoothed distribution and an exploration floor:
\begin{equation}
p(u'\mid \mathbf{h}_{t,c})=
(1-\epsilon)\operatorname{softmax}
\left(\frac{\mathbf{h}_{t,c}^\top\mathbf{z}_{u'}}{\tau_s}\right)
+\frac{\epsilon}{M}.
\end{equation}
The sampled candidates are then reordered by a course-fit score:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
b(u',c)=&
w_C C(u',c)-w_P P(u',c)\\
&-w_D D(u',c)-w_R R(u',c),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
s_{sample}(u',c)=
\cos(\mathbf{h}_{t,c},\mathbf{z}_{u'})
+\beta\operatorname{norm}(b(u',c)).
\end{equation}
The four terms \(C\), \(P\), \(D\), and \(R\) are the concept bonus, prerequisite gap, difficulty gap, and redundancy penalty defined below. This knowledge-guided sampling bias is applied to both cold and hot examples in the final run, so retrieval is filtered by educational plausibility as well as latent similarity.

An actor-critic policy selects a learner \(a_t\) from the sampled candidates. CKG-RL then updates the course state by taking a gradient step toward a mixture of the selected learner and the initial course embedding:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{t+1,c}=\mathbf{h}_{t,c}+\eta
\nabla_{\mathbf{h}_{t,c}}
\left[
(1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{h}_{t,c}^\top\mathbf{z}_{a_t}
+\alpha_t\mathbf{h}_{t,c}^\top\mathbf{e}_c
\right].
\end{equation}
The target coefficient is adaptive:
\begin{align}
\alpha_t=
\operatorname{clip}_{[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]}\Big(
&\alpha_{cold}\xi_c+\alpha_{hot}(1-\xi_c) \nonumber\\
&+\alpha_{step}\frac{t}{T-1}
-\alpha_{ent}\frac{H(\pi_t)}{\log N}
\Big).
\end{align}
Thus cold courses receive stronger target anchoring, later updates within the simulator horizon increase target guidance, and high-entropy actions reduce overconfident target drift. The simulator is not a real learner trajectory. It is a training-time mechanism for making the cold-course representation stable under plausible learner matches.

\subsection{Course-Knowledge Reward Design}
The reward terms convert educational constraints into simulator signals, reflecting the learning-resource literature's emphasis on context, sequencing, and learner-resource fit~\cite{dasilva2023educationalrs,rahayu2023learningpath}. Let \(\mathbf{s}_u\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{I}|}\) represent courses already seen by learner \(u\), \(n_u=\sum_{c'\in\mathcal{I}} s_{u,c'}\), \(\mathbf{A}^{pre}\) the course-level prerequisite matrix, and \(\mathbf{A}^{con}\) the course-concept overlap matrix. The prerequisite gap estimates missing prerequisite evidence:
\begin{equation}
P(u,c)=
\left[
1-\frac{\mathbf{s}_u^\top \mathbf{A}^{pre}_{c}}
{\max(1,\|\mathbf{A}^{pre}_{c}\|_1)}
\right]_0^1.
\end{equation}
A prerequisite-safe gate \(S_{pre}(u,c)\) is one when this gap is below the safety threshold. The concept match and bounded concept bonus are
\begin{equation}
M(u,c)=
\left[
\frac{\mathbf{s}_u^\top \mathbf{A}^{con}_{c}}{\max(1,n_u)}
\right]_0^1.
\end{equation}
The bounded concept bonus is
\begin{equation}
C(u,c)=
\left[
\frac{M(u,c)-\mu_C}{\mu_R-\mu_C}
\right]_0^1
\cdot S_{pre}(u,c)\cdot (1-\gamma_R R(u,c)).
\end{equation}
Course difficulty is estimated from inverse training popularity,
\begin{equation}
d_c=1-\frac{\log(1+p_c)}
{\max_{c'\in\mathcal{I}} \log(1+p_{c'})}.
\end{equation}
with learner readiness \(r_u=[n_u/n_{warm}]_0^1\) and gap \(D(u,c)=\max(0,d_c-r_u)\). When concept overlap is too high, the redundancy penalty is
\begin{equation}
R(u,c)=
\left[
\frac{M(u,c)-\mu_R}{1-\mu_R}
\right]_0^1.
\end{equation}

The step reward combines target preservation, progress, duplicate-action control, and the four course-knowledge terms:
\begin{align}
r_t=&-w_T\|\mathbf{h}_{t+1,c}-\mathbf{e}_c\|_2^2
+w_G G_t-w_{dup}\operatorname{Dup}_t \nonumber\\
&+w_C C(a_t,c)-w_P P(a_t,c) \nonumber\\
&-w_D D(a_t,c)-w_R R(a_t,c),
\end{align}
where \(G_t=\operatorname{clip}(\|\mathbf{h}_{t,c}-\mathbf{e}_c\|_2^2-\|\mathbf{h}_{t+1,c}-\mathbf{e}_c\|_2^2)\).

\subsection{Optimization}
Optimization combines policy learning with ranking supervision. The actor-critic policy is optimized with PPO and generalized advantage estimation~\cite{schulman2017ppo,schulman2015gae}:
\begin{equation}
\delta_t=r_t+\gamma V(\mathbf{s}_{t+1})-V(\mathbf{s}_t),
\quad
\hat{A}_t=\sum_{l\geq 0}(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l}.
\end{equation}
With probability ratio \(\rho_t(\theta)\), the clipped actor objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{actor}=
-\mathbb{E}_t\left[
\min(\rho_t\hat{A}_t,
\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon_p,1+\epsilon_p)\hat{A}_t)
\right].
\end{equation}
After the \(T\)-step simulator horizon, we denote the final course embedding as \(\mathbf{z}_{c,T}:=\mathbf{h}_{T,c}\). The ranking logit is
\begin{equation}
\ell(u,c)=\frac{\operatorname{norm}(\mathbf{z}_u)^\top
\operatorname{norm}(\mathbf{z}_{c,T})}{\tau}.
\end{equation}
The main ranking loss is a cross-entropy contrastive loss over the positive course and mixed hard/random in-batch negatives. Same-course and known-positive negatives are masked to reduce false negatives, a known concern in negative-sampling practice~\cite{ma2026negative}. The model also applies an auxiliary InfoNCE loss between the course ID embedding and the content embedding, plus a prerequisite auxiliary margin loss for high-violation negatives. These terms keep the ranking objective tied to observed learner-course pairs while PPO shapes the simulation policy. The final objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=
\mathcal{L}_{rank}
+\lambda_{aux}\mathcal{L}_{aux}
+\mathcal{L}_{ppo}
+\lambda_{pre}\mathcal{L}_{pre}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Implementation Details}
The implementation uses 128-dimensional learner and course embeddings, a 256-dimensional hidden layer, and 768-dimensional course content features. The MOOCCube/MOOCCubeX and COCO content features are BERT [CLS] representations~\cite{devlin2019bert}, using Chinese and English encoders respectively; Junyi is treated as an educational course generalization setting with processed course-content features. The simulator uses horizon \(T=5\) with update rate \(\eta=0.3\). At each step, it retrieves \(M=256\) users, samples \(N=20\) candidates with \(\tau_s=0.20\) and \(\epsilon=0.10\), and reorders candidates with knowledge-sampling weight \(\beta=0.20\). The educational reward weights are \(0.08\) for prerequisite penalty, \(0.04\) for concept bonus, \(0.03\) for difficulty penalty, and \(0.02\) for redundancy penalty. The target-anchor coefficients are \(\alpha_{cold}=0.35\), \(\alpha_{hot}=0.60\), \(\alpha_{step}=0.20\), \(\alpha_{ent}=0.20\), and \([\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]=[0.15,0.85]\). PPO uses two epochs, \(\gamma=0.90\), \(\lambda=0.95\), clip ratio \(0.2\), value clipping \(0.20\), and advantage normalization. The final strict course-cold runs train for 60 epochs with batch size 2048 and select checkpoints using cold course-macro validation behavior. The reported configuration uses only the components described in this section.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Dataset and Protocol}
The experiments use three educational recommendation settings, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:dataset-statistics}. The strict course-cold balanced protocol uses three random seeds, 2025, 2026, and 2027. Warm interactions are split 80/10/10 before cold-course reinsertion. Eligible cold courses are assigned to 20 popularity-balanced folds: one validation fold and two test folds, giving validation/test cold course ratios of 0.05/0.10. All interactions of validation and test cold courses are removed from training, while test users must retain training history, isolating course rather than user cold-start. The final course-macro evaluation contains about 68 MOOCCube cold courses, 71 Junyi cold courses, and 820 COCO cold courses per seed. After train-history masking, COCO uses about 6,048 warm candidate courses per seed.

The split is a controlled course-cold benchmark, not a chronological launch-time study. Primary metrics are full-ranking course-macro Recall@\(K\) and NDCG@\(K\), \(K \in \{5,10,20\}\). Hot course-macro metrics are auxiliary diagnostics. This separates zero-interaction generalization, full-catalog competition, and whether gains cover cold courses rather than concentrating on high-volume ones~\cite{bauer2024landscape,ekstrand2024distributional}.

\subsection{Research Questions}
The experiments answer four questions. \textbf{RQ1:} What strict course-cold full-ranking course-macro scores does CKG-RL obtain relative to the baselines? \textbf{RQ2:} Which exposure and pedagogical-risk patterns motivate the method components? \textbf{RQ3:} Which course-knowledge components contribute under the same protocol? \textbf{RQ4:} How stable are the gains under design variations, and what cost does CKG-RL introduce?

\subsection{Baselines}
To keep the comparison protocol-controlled, all baselines use the same course-cold splits, full-catalog candidate set, train-history masking, course-macro evaluator, and validation cold NDCG@10 checkpoint rule~\cite{sun2020rigorous,hidasi2023flaws}. We compare against four capability classes. \textbf{Interaction-only} baselines include Popularity, BPR~\cite{rendle2009bpr}, and LightGCN~\cite{he2020lightgcn}. They test whether learner-side history or graph propagation can rank target courses whose own training edges are removed. \textbf{Side-feature fallback} uses DropoutNet~\cite{volkovs2017dropoutnet}. \textbf{Content-transfer} uses CCFCRec~\cite{zhou2023ccfcrec}, ALDI~\cite{huang2023aldi}, and SEMCo~\cite{meehan2026semco}. \textbf{Graph reconstruction} uses CGRC~\cite{kim2024cgrc}, the closest baseline because it reconstructs cold-course structure from course content. DropoutNet, ALDI, and SEMCo are adapted from official implementations; CCFCRec uses a public-source static-HIN wrapper; and CGRC is reimplemented from the paper because no official code was available. For SEMCo, we replace only the data loader and final evaluator to match our strict course-cold splits and full-ranking course-macro protocol, while keeping its learner and sparse contrastive objective unchanged.

\subsection{Theoretical Complexity}
Let \(E\) be the number of training interactions, \(I\) the catalog size, \(Q\) the number of evaluation cases, and \(d\) the embedding dimension. CKG-RL adds a bounded simulator term \(O(E T(Md+Nd+H))\) during training, where \(T=5\), \(M=256\), and \(N=20\) in the reported runs. After course representations are cached, full-ranking inference uses the same dominant dot-product term \(O(QId)\) as embedding baselines. The supplement reports seed-level CIs, per-course gain diagnostics, and CGRC cost/timing coverage.

\subsection{RQ2: Exposure and Pedagogical-Risk Diagnostics}
The main-table ordering identifies the exposure side of the motivation. Popularity receives zero score for held-out cold courses, interaction-based representation learning lacks target-course edges, and content-transfer or graph-reconstruction methods still face warm courses with richer collaborative evidence. The diagnostic in Figure~\ref{fig:method-motivation}(a) shows that 30\% of CGRC seed-course cases have NDCG@10 no greater than 0.05, and 46\% are no greater than 0.10. Thus the strongest content-graph baseline still leaves many zero-interaction courses with little early exposure under full-catalog ranking.

The pedagogical-fit diagnostic added the second part. In the same cold-test setting, the mean prerequisite gap was 0.63, the mean difficulty gap was 0.45, and 68.6\% of seed-course cases received zero concept bonus. We do not interpret these measurements as evidence that each CGRC miss comes from a specific knowledge signal. Instead, the values indicated that cold MOOC targets often carried prerequisite, difficulty, and concept-continuity risks that generic content transfer left unmodeled. This observation motivated CKG-RL's use of prerequisite, concept, difficulty, and redundancy signals inside candidate construction and rewards rather than only as appended course features. The full catalog also mattered: sampled negatives could remove the warm-course competition that makes zero-interaction exposure difficult.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The experiments point to the evaluation unit as a source of hidden risk. Interaction-weighted metrics can improve when a model serves a few high-volume cold courses, even if many other cold courses receive no useful rank; distribution-aware evaluation is designed to expose this pattern~\cite{ekstrand2024distributional,bauer2024landscape}. Course-macro full ranking makes that failure mode visible. Course knowledge addresses a different part of the problem. Concept overlap supplies a semantic bridge, whereas prerequisite and difficulty signals add readiness-oriented structure.

Deployment validation is outside the scope of this offline study. The benchmarks isolate about 68 MOOCCube cold courses, 71 Junyi cold courses, and 820 COCO cold courses per seed, a smaller scale than industrial catalogs. Course relations and embeddings are dataset-specific; prerequisite and difficulty signals are proxies for readiness, not direct mastery estimates. Offline ranking does not measure learning outcomes or long-term impact, so deployment requires online and pedagogical validation~\cite{dasilva2023educationalrs,stray2024values}.

\section{Conclusion}
We studied strict course-cold MOOC recommendation, where target courses have no training interactions and full-ranking course-macro evaluation exposes failures hidden by interaction averages. CKG-RL combines content anchoring, forced-cold masking, retrieval-based simulation, and course-knowledge rewards. Across MOOCCube, Junyi, and COCO, it yields higher mean early-rank course-cold metrics under the same full-ranking course-macro protocol. The results indicate that reducing course-cold exposure risk requires both content transfer and educational constraints.

\section*{Ethical Statement}
Experiments use public MOOCCube/MOOCCubeX, Junyi, and COCO data plus derived course representations. Released artifacts should exclude personally identifiable information and follow dataset terms. Deployment requires online validation and monitoring for visibility, fairness, transparency, and consequential use~\cite{stray2024values,zhao2025fairnessdiversity}.
